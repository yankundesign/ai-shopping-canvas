## 1. For each platform: what AI shopping assistant or agentic feature exists, when did it launch, and what usage or revenue numbers have been disclosed?

### Takeaway
Every large US commerce platform now ships a first-party assistant, but only Amazon (400M+ users of Alexa for Shopping in 12 months, ~$12B incremental annualized sales claimed for 2025) and Walmart (Sparky usage +129% YoY, 40% larger baskets) disclose meaningful scale; everyone else reports growth multiples from a small base. Sales that originate in outside assistants remain tiny everywhere they are disclosed (under 1% of traffic or room nights at Etsy and Booking.com; eMarketer puts all US end-to-end agentic commerce at $1.44B for 2026).

## 2. How does the feature present options to the shopper (chat answers, cards, carousels, comparison views, lists/boards) and does it keep a persistent shortlist or comparison surface?

### Takeaway
The dominant pattern is a chat panel layered on the existing search and product-detail pages, returning prose plus product cards, with comparison offered on request rather than as a standing surface. Amazon is the furthest along: side-by-side comparison of products the shopper selects, generated buying guides, and agent access to existing Lists and registries. No platform I found has shipped a dedicated, persistent AI shortlist or comparison board; persistence is delegated to pre-existing constructs (Amazon Lists, Airbnb wishlists, carts).

- Amazon entry points (May 2026): the main search bar or a dedicated Alexa for Shopping chat window; voice, touch or both; mobile, desktop and Echo Show (fetched) — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/13/amazon-launches-an-ai-shopping-assistant-for-the-search-bar-powered-by-alexa/)

## 3. Which platforms open themselves to outside AI agents (feeds, APIs, MCP servers, protocol support) and which block or sue them? What are the stated reasons?

### Takeaway
The market has split into three postures: closed (Amazon blocks and litigates; eBay bans by contract unless it grants permission), permissioned-open (Walmart's Agent Gateway and agent profiles; Target, Etsy and Instacart through named partner integrations), and open infrastructure (Shopify with UCP, Catalog, hosted MCP servers and WebMCP checkout; Klarna's product protocol). The legal baseline shifted on 4 Aug 2026 when the Ninth Circuit vacated Amazon's injunction against Perplexity's Comet, holding that the user, not the agent vendor, is the one accessing the site; Amazon has since leaned on its Conditions of Use and technical blocks instead.

- Travel flows follow "discover in chat, transact on brand site" almost uniformly. Stated reasons are trust and post-booking service; the unstated one is retaining the customer record, loyalty link and ancillary upsell.
- On-site, the trend is away from one all-purpose trip chatbot (Expedia dropped Romie) and toward small agents attached to a specific decision point: compare these hotels, explain this property. That is a decision-support pattern, not a conversation pattern.
- Discoverability inside assistants is itself a problem: an installed app can be ignored by the host model, and paid placements already appear beside organic app results. Being integrated does not guarantee being shown.
- Airbnb's wishlist-based comparison and Expedia's compare agent are the two clearest travel examples of AI working over a user-curated shortlist rather than generating a fresh list each turn.

### Gaps

## 5. What do retailers say they fear or want from agentic commerce (loss of customer relationship, ads revenue, data, disintermediation)?

### Takeaway
On the record, retailers talk about trust, accuracy and "meeting customers where they are"; the fear of becoming a fulfilment back-end is voiced mainly by analysts and by retailers' actions (keeping checkout, requiring account linking, building gateways). The strongest hard evidence of retailer preference is behavioural: Walmart abandoned assistant-owned checkout after it converted at one-third the rate, OpenAI shelved Instant Checkout, and Amazon said it wants its own agent to be the primary way customers shop Amazon.

### Cited Findings
- Analyst framing (26 Jan 2026): Wharton's Kartik Hosanagar: "Whoever controls the agents now has the power", and retailers risk becoming "like a fulfillment company"; agents become "a new class of customer". Aptos's Nikki Baird on Google's pitch: "He's talking about Google owning and collecting all of the data...", so the retailer "loses so much context because it's outside of their realm" (fetched) — [Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/news/retails-ai-bet-risks-data-market-share/810202/)

- Counter-example where the retailer is the agent: independent brands objected to Amazon's Buy for Me listing them without consent and leaving returns to them, the same loss-of-control complaint large retailers make about outside agents (Jan 2026, snippet) — [Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/technology/brands-are-upset-that-buy-for-me-is-featuring-their-products-on-amazon-without-permission/)

### Inferences
- Retailers rarely name ad revenue or data loss as a fear in public. Those motives are attributed by analysts and journalists. The report should distinguish what executives said (trust, accuracy, security, customer choice) from what observers infer (ads, data, discovery ownership).
- Revealed preferences are consistent across retail and travel: keep the account, keep the checkout, keep the loyalty link. Walmart, Target, Expedia and Booking all require or route to their own sign-in and transaction.
- Retailers appear more comfortable with assistants as a discovery channel than as a buyer. Low consumer willingness to let agents purchase unreviewed (Okta, Shopify surveys) gives them cover to hold that line for now.
- The sharpest strategic difference is between retailers that sell attention (Amazon's ad business) and those that mostly sell goods (Walmart, Target): the former lose more when an agent skips the results page.
- A recurring want is identifiable, permissioned agents with user-set limits, which implies retailers expect to treat agents as a managed customer type with their own profile, not as anonymous traffic.

### Gaps
- No direct executive quote found in which a retailer names retail media revenue as the reason for restricting agents.
- Amazon's reasoning in its own court filings was not read directly; statements here come from press coverage.
- No retailer-side data on how agent-originated orders affect return rates, basket composition or ad yield.
- Sentiment from mid-size retailers and from sellers on marketplaces (beyond the Buy for Me complaints and Etsy seller backlash) was not researched.
- Deloitte and Gartner figures were taken from trade-press summaries, not the original reports.
