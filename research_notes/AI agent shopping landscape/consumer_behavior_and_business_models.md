# Consumer behavior and economics of shopping through AI assistants and agents (as of 24 November 2026)

Reader notes that apply to every section:

- "Vendor" = the publisher sells products or services that benefit from the finding (Adobe, Salesforce, Similarweb, Riskified, impact.com, Accenture, McKinsey, Bain, Synchrony, UserTesting, Couponfollow). "Independent" = academic, non-profit, or analyst/UX research firm with no direct product stake (Pew, Baymard, NN/g, Forrester, eMarketer, peer-reviewed papers). Trade bodies (IAB, IATA) sit in between.
- "[search summary]" means the figure came from a search-result digest and I did not open the primary page. Treat those as leads to verify, not settled numbers.

- Cyber Week 2026 runs 24 to 30 November, so no measured 2026 holiday results exist yet. Everything for holiday 2026 is a forecast.

## 1. What are the latest adoption figures for AI-assisted shopping, and how do AI referral conversion rates compare with other channels?

### Takeaway
Using AI for product research is now a majority or near-majority behavior in US surveys (roughly 36% to 81% depending on wording), and AI-referred retail traffic is growing fast with strong per-visit value in Adobe's 2026 data, but it is still a small slice of total traffic and the one peer-reviewed study (older data) found ChatGPT referrals converting worse than Google search. Travel is the most AI-penetrated category (about half of travelers); I found no reliable data for event tickets.

### Inferences
- The defensible reading is "AI is now a mainstream research and comparison step, and a minor transaction channel." About 1.5% of US e-commerce completing on AI platforms (eMarketer) sits next to 21% "influenced" (Salesforce); the gap is definitional, not a contradiction.
- Adobe's conversion advantage for AI traffic is recent (it was negative in August 2025) and has widened every few months. The likely mechanism is selection: people click through from an assistant after they have already narrowed their choice, so the visit is late-funnel. This is consistent with the IAB finding in section 2 that shoppers go to retailer sites to verify and buy.
- The academic result and the Adobe result are not directly comparable: different time windows (the academic data ends July 2025, right before Adobe's series flips positive), different site mixes, and different attribution. Present both; do not cite only the favorable one.
- Survey adoption numbers vary by a factor of two because of question wording ("ever used AI to research a product" versus "use generative chatbots for shopping"). The lower Adobe figure (36% use chatbots for shopping) and Bain's 30% to 45% are the more conservative anchors.

### Gaps
- No measured holiday 2026 results yet (Cyber Week began today). Adobe and Salesforce recaps should appear from late November through early January.
- Adobe does not publish AI traffic as an absolute share of retail visits in the materials I retrieved, only growth rates.
- I did not retrieve a 2026 device split for AI-referred traffic. Adobe's 2025 AI traffic report covers this and should be checked: https://business.adobe.com/assets/pdfs/resources/reports/ai-traffic-report/ai-sourced-traffic-insights-2025.pdf

- No reliable data found on AI use for event or ticket purchases specifically.

## 2. What do usability studies and surveys say is missing or frustrating when shopping in a chat interface, especially on phones? Do people still want to browse, see options visually, and compare side by side?

### Takeaway
The consistent evidence is that chat helps people narrow and compare but adds verification work and does not replace browsing: shoppers leave the chat to check prices, variants, reviews, and availability on retailer pages, struggle with prompting, and hit price and stock errors. OpenAI's own March 2026 pivot (de-emphasizing in-chat checkout, adding visual browsing and side-by-side product views) is the strongest revealed-preference signal that plain chat was insufficient. Phone-specific usability evidence on chat shopping is thin.

### Cited Findings

**Observed behavior and usability studies**
- IAB and Talk Shoppe (trade body study; 450+ screen-recorded AI shopping sessions plus a 600-person US survey; reported 28 October 2025) [older than 12 months]: shoppers averaged 1.6 steps before AI and 3.8 after; 95% took extra steps to feel confident before ending a session; 78% visited a retailer or marketplace during the journey; 32% clicked directly from the AI tool to a retailer; retailer-site visits rose from 20% before AI to 50% after. On retailer pages they checked prices and deals, variants, reviews, and availability. Only 46% fully trusted AI shopping recommendations. Trust was lost through missing links or sources, mismatched specs or pricing, outdated availability, and recommendations that ignored budget or compatibility. — [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/trust-in-ai-shopping-is-limited-as-shoppers-verify-on-websites/559497/)
- UserTesting (vendor; qualitative, n=10 adults in US, Canada, Australia shopping for a dishwasher with their preferred chatbot; summer 2025) [older than 12 months, very small sample]: AI-generated comparison tables with pictures and feature bullets were popular, and participants valued choosing the comparison criteria themselves. Bots misreported prices or changed them mid-conversation (one product moved from AU$1,198 to about AU$1,499), and the chatbot's explanations "sounded like excuses." Participants shopped in two phases (pick a model, then find a retailer and price) and the journey "often broke down" at the handoff to retailers. Many would only buy from a familiar retailer. — [UserTesting](https://www.usertesting.com/blog/ai-assisted-shopping-customer-journey)
- Adobe (vendor; 2026; n=1,000+ US shoppers): nearly one in five shoppers abandoned an AI shopping request because they did not know how to ask for what they wanted. [search summary; page timed out] — [Adobe](https://business.adobe.com/blog/the-ai-shopping-gap)
- NN/g (independent): on retail sites with AI chat assistants (Home Depot, Turo tested), the main problem was discoverability. Regular customers had never noticed the chat feature; icons lacked labels; vague opening messages left users unsure what the assistant could do. [search summary] — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/site-ai-chatbot/)
- NIM (Nuremberg Institute for Market Decisions, non-profit) experiment with a Zalando-style shop: shoppers using a conversational assistant made choices of similar quality to those using a standard shop but took longer to decide. [search summary; PDF did not parse, design details unverified] — [NIM Insights Vol. 8](https://www.nim.org/fileadmin/PUBLIC/3_NIM_Publikationen/NIM_INSIGHTS_Research_Magazin/VOL_8/Einzelartikel/INS_V8_-_3_From_Clicks_to_Conversations.pdf)
- TU Delft thesis research with Decathlon (academic): characterizes conversation as a sequential, linear dialogue that mirrors older chat paradigms; reports a tension between spatial memory and natural-language expression, and unclear system boundaries. [search summary; not opened] — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_caed96ab-8651-417c-a055-f7a6f3314f82?preview=1)
- A press headline claims AI shopping assistants are "wrong 86% of the time." I did not open the study; treat as unverified. — [Tech.co](https://tech.co/?p=367919)

**Survey evidence on what shoppers want from AI versus traditional browsing**
- Baymard (independent UX research; survey of 2,273 US online shoppers, data dated June 2026, published 27 October 2026): shoppers prefer AI chatbots for low-stakes research, namely comparing product features (63%), checking stock (59%), and personalized recommendations (56%). They prefer a human for damaged or incorrect items (69%), late or missing orders (67%), and shipping or billing mistakes (66%). 60% say knowing a product image is AI-generated would reduce purchase confidence (17% would not buy at all). Baymard reads images as a verification tool for shoppers. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/ai-engagement-quantitative-ux-insights-2026)
- Coverage of the same Baymard study says the vast majority of shoppers still rely on traditional site navigation and exploration, with only a subgroup willing to use AI for shopping. [search summary] — [Retail Insider](https://retail-insider.com/?p=212314)
- impact.com (vendor): asked how they would use a personalized AI shopping agent, 24% would replace their product search methods entirely, 36% would use it as a secondary tool for ideas before verifying manually, 23% only for complex or high-stakes purchases, and 17% would not change habits. 41% of AI-active consumers usually or always click the AI's embedded link to a brand or retailer site before deciding. Most valued agent features: personalized fit 27%, verified reviews 24%, automatic savings 23%, full automation 15%. — [impact.com](https://impact.com/resources/reports/ai-driven-buyer-journey/personalized-ai-shopping-agents-and-auto-buying/)

**Platform behavior as evidence**
- On 24 March 2026 OpenAI said its initial Instant Checkout "did not offer the level of flexibility" it wanted and shifted focus to product discovery. It rolled out visual product browsing, image upload to find similar items, conversational refinement, and products shown side by side with price, reviews, and features, to Free, Go, Plus, and Pro users. Merchants can use their own checkout or build ChatGPT apps. — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/powering-product-discovery-in-chatgpt/)

**Mobile context**
- Adobe forecasts mobile at 57.4% of US online holiday spend in 2026 (56.4% in 2025), with a record 63% on peak days. — [Adobe](https://news.adobe.com/news/2026/09/adobe-us-holiday-shopping-season-to-hit-record)
- Baymard observed that mobile users rarely use multiple tabs while browsing, unlike desktop users. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/provide-comparison-features)

### Inferences
- The recurring failure points are (a) not knowing how to prompt, (b) no persistent, visible shortlist or comparison surface (the linear chat scroll buries earlier options), (c) stale or wrong prices and availability, (d) missing sources and links, and (e) a broken handoff to the retailer. A shopping canvas that holds candidates visibly, shows live price and stock with timestamps, and links out cleanly addresses (b) through (e) directly.
- Comparison is the single most-wanted AI shopping task in three separate sources (Baymard 63%, impact.com 40% past-30-day use, Bizrate 26% primary reason), and the comparison table was the most-liked output in the UserTesting sessions. That is a strong argument for making structured comparison the core artifact rather than free-text answers.
- Mobile is where most spend happens, and mobile is where the tab-based comparison workaround is least available. Combined with a narrow chat viewport, the phone is plausibly where the unmet need for a compact shortlist and comparison view is greatest. This is an inference; I found no study that tested chat shopping on phones directly.
- AI may lengthen rather than shorten the journey (1.6 to 3.8 steps; longer decision time in the NIM experiment). A product should not promise speed; it should promise confidence and less re-work.

### Gaps
- No NN/g or Baymard qualitative study specifically on shopping inside general chat assistants (ChatGPT, Gemini) on phones was found. Baymard's full AI engagement study (20+ insights, including limits of AI purchasing authority) is behind its paywall.
- The Adobe "AI shopping gap" page timed out twice; its numbers come from a search digest.
- The NIM and TU Delft findings need a direct read before being quoted in a final report.
- The two richest behavioral studies (IAB, UserTesting) are from 2025 and predate ChatGPT's March 2026 visual shopping update, so they may overstate current friction inside ChatGPT.

## 3. What does research say about comparison and shortlisting behavior in e-commerce (number of tabs, products compared, wishlists and carts as holding areas)?

### Takeaway
Established UX research shows shoppers improvise shortlists: desktop users open products in multiple tabs, phone users rarely do, and many people use the cart as a holding and comparison area rather than a purchase commitment. Dedicated comparison tools are uncommon and often poorly built. I found no reliable published count of how many tabs or products a typical shopper compares.

### Cited Findings
- Baymard (independent): to compare products, desktop users open each item in its own tab, then have to tile windows or hold specs in memory; mobile users were observed to rarely use multiple tabs. Baymard recommends sites with spec-driven products always provide comparison features, and a well-built one lets users see every spec for up to 5 products at once. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/provide-comparison-features)
- Baymard: compare controls shown only on hover get overlooked; poorly designed comparison tools frustrate users to the point of exiting the feature. Recommended fixes include hiding identical attributes, grouping attributes, sticky column headers, and horizontal row styling. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/user-friendly-comparison-tools)
- Only 38% of the top 60 e-commerce sites Baymard benchmarks have a dedicated comparison tool. [search summary] — [Baymard benchmark](https://baymard.com/ecommerce-product-lists/benchmark/page-types/comparison-tool)
- NN/g (independent; research across 49 e-commerce sites; published 4 November 2018) [older than 12 months, by a wide margin]: shoppers frequently used the cart as a "holding area" to compare items and save products across sessions. Wishlists were underused because they required registration or list naming, and the word "Wishlist" implied gift sharing. The cart signaled "I might want that." NN/g advises not treating cart abandonment as pure loss. On mobile, save-for-later controls hidden behind swipes or dropdowns were hard to discover. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wishlist-or-cart/)
- Baymard's compiled cart abandonment average is about 70% across 50 studies, and roughly 43% of US abandoners say they were "just browsing / not ready to buy." Baymard's page combines research from different years. [search summary] — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/cart-abandonment-rate-statistics)
- Cart abandonment by device, Dynamic Yield (vendor) data for July 2026 as reported secondhand: mobile 79.98%, tablet 75.81%, desktop 71.17%. [search summary] — [Red Stag Fulfillment](https://redstagfulfillment.com/percentage-of-online-shoppers-abandon-their-cart/)
- In qualitative sessions, shoppers using a chatbot split the task into picking a model and then finding a retailer and price, and liked being able to set their own comparison criteria. [older than 12 months; n=10] — [UserTesting](https://www.usertesting.com/blog/ai-assisted-shopping-customer-journey)

### Inferences
- The cart-as-holding-area and tabs-as-shortlist behaviors are workarounds for a missing cross-retailer shortlist. Both are single-retailer (cart) or single-device and volatile (tabs). A cross-retailer, persistent shortlist with side-by-side comparison is the unmet job.
- Because a large share of "abandonment" is deliberate parking, a holding area is a normal step in the purchase process and not a sign of failure. A canvas that makes parking explicit matches what people already do.
- Baymard's ceiling of about 5 products in a usable comparison view is a reasonable design constraint for a comparison surface, especially on phones.
- Low friction matters more than features: NN/g found people avoided wishlists that demanded sign-up or naming. A shortlist should work with no account and no setup.

### Gaps
- I found no credible statistic for the average number of tabs opened or products compared per purchase. Figures circulating on vendor blogs were not traceable to primary research and are excluded.
- The best evidence on cart-as-holding-area is from 2018. The behavior is widely re-observed, but I did not find a 2025 or 2026 primary study quantifying it.
- No academic HCI paper (CHI, CSCW) on tab-based comparison shopping was retrieved in this pass; this is a search gap, not evidence of absence.

## 4. How willing are consumers to let an AI agent pay on their behalf, by category and price level?

### Takeaway
Across 2026 surveys, consumers accept AI for research, comparison, and even cart building, but only about 9% to 16% will let an agent complete and pay for a purchase on its own, and comfort appears to have fallen during 2026. Willingness concentrates in low-cost, routine replenishment (grocery, household) and drops for identity or enjoyment purchases (apparel, hotels, experiences).

### Cited Findings

**Independent or analyst**
- Forrester (survey fielded April 2026; online adults in US, UK, Canada; sample size not public; published 15 June 2026): about three-quarters are uncomfortable letting an AI agent complete a purchase and pay autonomously, even with preset spending limits and rules, and trust is low regardless of which company provides the agent. Recommending and comparing is generally acceptable; autonomously filling a cart is accepted by some; completing payment is rarely accepted, and then mainly for routine, low-cost items under strict rules. Consumers expect the AI platform, not the retailer, to be responsible for agent mistakes. Context: only about one in four US online adults used Apple Pay in the past three months after more than a decade. — [Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/consumers-arent-ready-to-delegate-payments-to-ai-agents/)
- eMarketer cut its AI-platform commerce forecast in 2026, citing consumers' "tepid" interest in having an agent buy for them. [search summary] — [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/retailers-hold-ai-commerce-advantage-over-chatgpt)
- Baymard's June 2026 survey (n=2,273 US) covers "limits of AI purchasing authority" and "responsibility for AI purchasing errors," but the figures are not in the free article. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/ai-engagement-quantitative-ux-insights-2026)

**Vendor or consultancy**
- Accenture 2026 Consumer Pulse (n=25,590, 16 countries; reported June 2026): 74% would delegate routine tasks (deal negotiation, complaints, renewals, reorders); 32% would accept delegated decision-making where the agent picks and the consumer approves before payment; 9% would allow autonomous purchasing within defined boundaries; 12% are open to autonomous decisions at the payment stage. Recurring services rank highest at every delegation stage; lifestyle and travel drop sharply as autonomy rises; consumers want control over choices tied to identity or enjoyment (hotels, clothing, experiences). Desired guardrails: data safeguards, configurable permissions, instant override, clear recourse, platform reputation, perceived neutrality. The widely quoted "74% trust an agent more than their best friend to make a purchase" comes from the same study and measures something different from the 9%. — [AI News](https://www.artificialintelligence-news.com/news/ai-shopping-agents-consumer-trust-accenture-report/)
- Deloitte (n=4,000 US consumers, fielded August to September; reported ahead of holiday shopping): only 16% trust AI agents to complete purchases for them. [search summary; survey year not confirmed] — [Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/commerce/consumers-arent-ready-to-let-ai-do-their-holiday-shopping-yet/)

### Inferences
- The approval step is the product. The jump from 9% (autonomous) to 32% (agent picks, human approves) to majority acceptance (agent researches and compares) in Accenture, plus Forrester's ladder, says a design where the agent prepares and the human makes the final choice fits where 85%+ of consumers are today.
- Numbers above 60% ("70% say yes to AI agents," "69% would allow in at least one category") come from AI-active samples or loosely worded questions. For general-population willingness to let an agent pay unsupervised, 9% to 16% is the range to use.
- Price evidence is weak but directional: acceptance clusters at low-ticket replenishment, and one vendor survey suggests resistance starts at very low amounts (about $25). Do not present a precise price curve; none exists in the sources.
- Trust may be moving the wrong way in 2026 (Riskified tracker, eMarketer downgrade), plausibly because of publicized agent errors and the Phia affiliate episode (section 5). A tool that is transparent about what the agent did has a credibility advantage.

### Gaps
- No source gives willingness by explicit price bands and category together. The $25 figure is secondhand from a fraud vendor.
- Forrester's sample size and exact percentages are client-only.
- Deloitte's field year needs confirmation from the primary report.
- No data found on willingness to let agents buy event tickets specifically; travel evidence is limited to Accenture's "sharper drop" statement and a Riskified summer travel survey I did not retrieve.

## 5. What are the real unit economics available to an independent shopping surface (affiliate rates, in-chat take rates, ads, subscriptions, infrastructure fees, attribution problems, and what has worked for small open-source tools)?

### Takeaway
For an independent surface the realistic revenue line is affiliate commission of roughly 1% to 4.5% on mainstream Amazon categories (up to 10% in a few niches), less a 25% network cut if using an aggregator, and that line is fragile because agent-driven journeys often produce no trackable click. The big platforms are converging on advertising (ChatGPT Ads passed a $1B run rate in 2026) while keeping checkout fees low or zero, so transaction take rates are not a dependable model to copy. ### Inferences
- Illustrative arithmetic (mine, from the rates above): a $100 Amazon basket in a 3% to 4% category yields $3 to $4; through an aggregator at a 75% share, $2.25 to $3. A $600 TV at 2% yields $12. Groceries at 1% yield almost nothing. At these rates an independent surface needs either very high purchase volume or direct merchant programs with higher rates (the best affiliate rates sit outside Amazon, but I did not retrieve a category benchmark).
- Affiliate income is structurally at odds with an agent-agnostic canvas in two ways. First, if a third-party agent does the browsing, there may be no tagged click to attribute. Second, the tool must never rewrite or overwrite links silently; Phia shows that networks will suspend for this and the reputational damage spreads to the category. The safe pattern is a visible, human-clicked outbound link with per-recommendation disclosure.
- The human-clicks-out pattern is also where the evidence says users are (41% usually click through; 78% visit a retailer in the IAB study), so a "prepare in canvas, user clicks to retailer" flow preserves both attribution and user preference. An autonomous-checkout flow would forfeit most affiliate revenue under current tracking.
- Amazon's API gate (reported 10 sales per 30 days to keep Creators API access) and 1-hour price cache rule are real operational constraints for a small tool: without API access, price data must come from scraping, which raises the legal exposure in section 6.
- Platform economics have settled, for now, on "free or cheap checkout, monetize with ads." OpenAI retreated from a 4% take-rate model within about six months while ads reached a $1B run rate. An independent tool cannot copy the ad model without scale, and should not plan around taking a transaction fee.
- For an open-source tool, the monetization paths consistent with the evidence are: (1) a hosted or synced version (accounts, cross-device shortlists, price tracking) as the paid tier; (2) clearly disclosed affiliate links as incidental revenue; (3) sponsorship as a minor supplement. This is an inference from general open-source patterns; I found no shopping-specific open-source success case.

## 6. What legal and platform risks would an agent-agnostic shopping canvas face?

### Takeaway
The law shifted in favor of user-directed agents in August 2026 when the Ninth Circuit vacated Amazon's injunction against Perplexity's Comet, reasoning that the user, not the agent maker, was accessing Amazon. That ruling is preliminary and narrow, the case continues, and major platforms (Amazon, eBay, Shopify) have written contractual and technical bans on unapproved buy-for-me agents, so the practical risk is account bans, blocking, affiliate termination, and contract claims more than computer-crime liability.

### Cited Findings

**Amazon v. Perplexity (Comet)**
- Amazon sent Perplexity a cease-and-desist in November 2025 over Comet's agentic shopping on Amazon. Amazon alleges both companies had agreed to pause agentic shopping in 2024 and that Perplexity later re-enabled it by disguising the agent as a standard Chrome browser. — [Engadget, 4 Aug 2026](https://engadget.com/2230471/perplexity-has-successfully-overturned-amazon-injunction-on-its-ai-shopping-bot)
- In March 2026 Judge Maxine Chesney (N.D. Cal.) granted a preliminary injunction, finding Comet accessed Amazon accounts "with the Amazon user's permission, but without authorization by Amazon." — [Bloomberg, 10 Mar 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-10/amazon-wins-court-order-blocking-perplexity-s-ai-shopping-bots); [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/03/10/judge-blocks-perplexitys-ai-bot-from-shopping-on-amazon-in-early-test-of-agentic-commerce/)
- On 4 August 2026 the Ninth Circuit vacated the injunction. Because Comet acts at the user's direction, the court treated the Comet user, not Perplexity, as the one accessing Amazon's servers, so the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act claim and the California CDAFA claim were unlikely to succeed, and Amazon's harm did not justify an injunction. Described as the first federal appellate ruling on whether agents acting for users can access online platforms. Amazon said it "respectfully disagree[s]" and was evaluating next steps. — [Engadget](https://engadget.com/2230471/perplexity-has-successfully-overturned-amazon-injunction-on-its-ai-shopping-bot)
- Rehearing was declined on 10 September 2026. Amazon then filed an amended complaint aimed at the iOS version of Comet, alleging the iPhone app sends user cookies to Perplexity's cloud servers. As of early November 2026 the case remained active in district court. [search summary] — [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/perplexity-ai-shopping-agent-returns-amazon/)

**Platform terms and technical blocking**
- eBay updated its User Agreement on 20 January 2026 (effective 20 February 2026) to bar buy-for-me agents, LLM-driven bots, and any end-to-end flow that places orders without human review unless eBay approves. It had added a "Robot & Agent Policy" to robots.txt in late 2025 and later explicit blocks on bots from Perplexity, Anthropic, Amazon, and others. eBay warns that unauthorized automated checkout may lead to legal action. [search summary] — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/22/ebay_updates_legalese_to_ban/); [EcommerceBytes](https://www.ecommercebytes.com/2026/01/21/ebay-bans-ai-shopping-agents-updates-arbitration-provision/); [Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/technology/ebay-adds-new-ai-agent-policy-to-its-website/)
- Shopify's "Robots & Agent" policy prohibits automated buy-for-me agents that complete payment "without a final human review step" and directs "legitimate integrators" to its official checkout kit. One report says Shopify later softened this; unverified. [search summary] — [Retail TouchPoints](https://www.retailtouchpoints.com/topics/digital-commerce/shopify-adds-guardrails-to-control-agentic-purchases)
- Amazon's seller-side Business Solutions Agreement added an Agent Policy effective 4 March 2026 with requirements for AI and automated systems, and bars using Amazon materials to develop or improve AI models. [search summary] — [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/amazons-new-ai-agent-rules-shake-up-sellers-before-march-4-deadline/)
- eMarketer flags retailer pushback as a factor that could slow AI shopping tools in 2026. [search summary] — [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/ai-shopping-tools-gain-traction-retailer-pushback-could-cloud-2026-progress)

**Affiliate and disclosure exposure**
- Amazon Associates can terminate immediately for policy breaches, bans use of program content for LLM training, and restricts link formatting and price caching (details and sourcing in section 5). — [Amazon Associates Operating Agreement](https://affiliate-program.amazon.com/help/operating/agreement)
- impact.com suspended Phia after hidden-tab affiliate link overwriting was documented; the same extension had been found in November 2025 collecting browsing data beyond what its shopping function needed. — [The Impression](https://theimpression.com/phias-affiliate-fraud-scandal-raises-questions-for-ai-shopping-agents/)
- FTC endorsement rules on material connections apply to affiliate-funded recommendations (section 5). — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/documents/bus71-ftcs-revised-endorsement-guideswhat-people-are-asking)

**Consumer-side liability expectations**
- Consumers expect the AI platform to be held responsible when an agent makes a purchasing mistake. — [Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/consumers-arent-ready-to-delegate-payments-to-ai-agents/)

### Inferences
- The Ninth Circuit's logic (the user is the one accessing the site) is most protective of architectures where the agent runs locally in the user's own browser session at the user's direction. Amazon's amended complaint targets the opposite pattern: user cookies or credentials leaving the device for a vendor's cloud. A canvas that never holds retailer credentials and never drives checkout server-side stays on the safer side of that line.
- All three platform policies single out the same act: completing payment without a final human review. A canvas where the agent assembles options and the human clicks through and pays on the retailer's own site falls outside the stated prohibition and also matches consumer preference (section 4). This is the lowest-risk design.
- Agent-agnostic helps and hurts. It helps because the canvas itself does not crawl or transact; the user's chosen agent does. It hurts because the canvas inherits whatever each agent does to obtain product data, and displaying scraped prices or images can still breach retailer terms or affiliate rules (for example Amazon's API-only pricing requirement).
- The durable risks are contractual and commercial (blocked user agents, banned accounts, affiliate termination, API access thresholds), which a small open-source project cannot litigate. Official feeds and protocols (ACP product feeds, UCP, Shopify Catalog, affiliate APIs) are the sanctioned route, and they favor integrators that retailers have approved.
- Disclosure and transparency are cheap insurance: per-recommendation affiliate disclosure, no hidden tabs, no link rewriting, and minimal data collection directly avoid the conduct that got Phia suspended.

### Gaps
- I did not read the Ninth Circuit opinion or the district court order; the reasoning is from press reports. The lawsuit's original filing date was reported inconsistently in the sources I saw and should be checked against the docket.
- Which claims remain live (trespass to chattels, breach of contract, CDAFA) was not confirmed.
- Shopify's and Amazon's primary policy texts were not retrieved; wording is from trade coverage.
- I did not research scraping case law (for example hiQ v. LinkedIn, Meta v. Bright Data), ticket-specific law such as the US BOTS Act, state AI or privacy statutes, or UK and EU rules. These matter if the tool touches ticketing or stores personal data.
- No evidence found of a retailer suing an open-source project or a pure display-layer tool; all documented disputes involve agents that log in and transact.

### Inferences
- Credibility ranking, in my judgment: eMarketer's figures are the most conservative and the most testable because they count sales completed on named platforms and have been revised against observed data. Morgan Stanley and Bain are mid-range and depend on a broad "influenced" definition. McKinsey's $1T and ARK's $8T are upper bounds built on "orchestrated" or "mediated" revenue and adoption assumptions that 2026 consumer-trust data (section 4) does not yet support.
- The top-down forecasts all predate or ignore three 2026 developments that cut against fast autonomous checkout: OpenAI's Instant Checkout retreat, declining or flat consumer comfort with agent payments, and platform bans on unapproved buy-for-me agents. They are still consistent with heavy AI involvement in discovery and comparison.
- Consultancies and vendors publishing these numbers sell agentic commerce strategy or software. That does not make them wrong, but it explains why "influenced" definitions are preferred. Salesforce's 2025 miss (forecast $73B, actual $67B) is small, which suggests near-term vendor forecasts are roughly reliable on their own definitions even if those definitions are generous.
- For planning, the supportable statement is: by 2030 AI will touch a large share of online shopping journeys, while agent-completed transactions are more likely in the mid-single-digit to roughly 10% share of US e-commerce than at 25%.

### Gaps
- Morgan Stanley's page timed out; its date, survey sample, and assumptions were not verified.
- Gartner's primary prediction was not located.
- eMarketer's revised tables are paywalled; the 2030 dollar figure is inferred from a URL slug.
- No forecast was found that isolates the "AI-assisted research, human-completed purchase" segment, which is the segment most relevant to a shopping canvas.
- No forecasts specific to UK, Canada, or Australia were collected.
